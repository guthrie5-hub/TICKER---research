## Investment policy statement mapping

An investment policy statement (IPS) is the written guide for how an investor’s assets should be managed. It separates desired outcomes from the rules that limit how those outcomes may be pursued, then assigns a process for monitoring compliance.

| IPS element | Meridian statements | Application |
|---|---|---|
| Time horizon and implementation | S1 | The reserve has a seven-year horizon and is rebalanced annually. |
| Return objective | S2 | The stated goal is CPI + 4% net of costs over rolling five-year periods. |
| Performance reference | S4 | The 60% global-equity/40% U.S. investment-grade-bond benchmark measures performance but does not require a 60/40 portfolio. |
| Liquidity and spending constraints | S3, S8 | The reserve must cover four quarterly distributions with eligible liquid assets and keep at least 90% redeemable within five business days. |
| Investment restrictions | S5, S6, S7 | The portfolio must be long-only, keep nonexempt single-company exposure at or below 8%, and remain within the turnover limit unless approved. |
| Risk policy needing definition | S9 | The stated 10% calendar-year-loss limit needs a measurement method, monitoring date, and breach response before it can be enforced. |
| Missing optimization instruction | S10 | The CIO must specify what the pilot is intended to optimize. |

## Candidate D — checker attack

Candidate D holds 100% Treasury 10-year notes. It passes every coded check because the holding is reserve-eligible, daily liquid, and exempt from the issuer cap.

The objection holds because passing liquidity, concentration, and long-only rules does not show that the portfolio can reasonably meet the CPI + 4% net-of-costs objective. S2 decides the objection: expected return and risk must still be evaluated against the stated return objective.

## CIO question before optimization

**Question:** What should the pilot optimize: expected return above CPI + 4%, closeness to the 60/40 benchmark, or minimizing the chance of a calendar-year loss beyond 10%?

**Portfolio-changing answer:** If the CIO prioritizes minimizing losses and defines the 10% rule as a hard limit, the portfolio should use less global equity and more cash or Treasuries than Candidate A. If the CIO prioritizes expected real return, a portfolio can retain more equity exposure.

## Reflection

A portfolio can pass every checkable rule and still be wrong because the checks test only the rules that have been translated into measurable inputs. Candidate D shows the gap: it passes the coded liquidity and concentration constraints but may not support Meridian’s return objective.

I was least sure about S9. It sounds like a hard loss constraint, but without a defined return measure, monitoring point, or stated consequence of breach, it cannot yet be tested or enforced as one.
